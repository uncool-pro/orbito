# Directions for bold variants

The pool the bold variants are drawn from (see `SKILL.md`). Each direction is a **device**, not a finished style: it says what carries the screen, never which font or colour to use. Those come from the product's inventory and the brand. Directions are numbered only so they can be drawn; the number carries no order.

When you add a direction, keep the same shape and never name a specific typeface, colour or product to copy.

1. **Type as the interface.** The most important figure or word on each screen is set very large and does the work an image or a card would do; everything else is small and quiet. Suits products with one number or one status people check often. Risk: long labels and translations; test the longest real string.

2. **Colour fields.** Screens are built from a few large flat areas of colour instead of cards on a grey ground; the field changes with the state or the section. Suits products with clear modes or moments (morning / evening, draft / sent). Risk: contrast of text on every field; check each pair.

3. **The object as the layout.** A physical thing from the product's world gives the shape of the screen: a ticket, a label, a receipt, a dial, a map fold. Suits products whose users handle that object in real life. Risk: the metaphor fighting platform conventions; keep standard navigation.

4. **Dense and precise.** More on the screen than usual, in a strict grid, small but readable type and very little decoration, like a good timetable. Suits expert users and long sessions. Risk: first-time users; check the empty and first-run states.

5. **One continuous surface.** No cards and no boxes: content sits directly on one ground, separated only by space and type size; hierarchy comes from scale. Suits calm, reading-heavy products. Risk: everything looking equally important; one element per screen must clearly lead.

6. **Photography first.** Real pictures of the product's world (the user's own items, places, people) take most of each screen, with the interface laid over or beside them. Suits products where the content is visual. Risk: the mockup needs believable pictures; plan their source with the client.

7. **Shape language.** One unusual shape, used everywhere and only that one: fully round, sharp square, a cut corner, a tab. It becomes the product's signature. Suits young brands without a strong logo. Risk: it turning into an ornament; the shape should hold content, not decorate it.

8. **Motion as structure.** Screens are connected by movement that explains where things come from and go (a card expands into the detail, a list item flies into the basket). Static screens stay simple. Suits flows with clear steps. Risk: it cannot be judged on a still screenshot; show it in the prototype and keep it short.

9. **Dark and calm.** A dark ground, light type, colour only on data and status, no glow. Suits products used at night, in the field or for long monitoring. Risk: looking like every dark template; the idea sentence must say what makes it this product's.

10. **Handmade.** A trace of the hand in one controlled place: drawn icons, a hand-lettered heading, a sketched illustration in empty states. The rest stays strict. Suits personal, hobby and care products. Risk: looking unfinished; limit it to one kind of element.

11. **Tool-like.** The product looks like an instrument: visible controls, clear states, labelled values, a sense of precision (a scale, a counter, a switch). Suits products that measure or control something. Risk: coldness; the product's words and objects should warm it.

12. **Editorial rhythm.** Screens read like a well-set page: a strong title, a lead sentence, varied blocks with different shapes from section to section, one large image or figure per screen. Suits content and guidance products. Risk: too much text for a task screen; keep task screens short.

13. **Playful scale.** Unexpected size contrasts: a huge illustration or number next to very small controls; elements that overlap the edge of the screen on purpose. Suits consumer products with a light tone. Risk: touch targets and readability; never shrink the controls themselves.

14. **Status as colour.** The whole screen's colour comes from the state of the main object (healthy / needs attention / overdue), so the state is read before any word. Suits products built around monitoring something. Risk: colour-only meaning; every state also needs a word or a shape.
